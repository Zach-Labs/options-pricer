Section 1

The kink, and why volatility is the product

A call is the right, not the obligation, to buy at K by time T. "Right, not obligation" is the whole mathematical content of the word option: it is what puts the max( , 0) into the payoff.

call payoff = max(S_T - K, 0) put payoff = max(K - S_T, 0)

Both are continuous but not differentiable at S_T = K. That single kink is the source of everything hard and everything profitable here.

Both payoffs are also convex. Jensen's inequality says E[f(X)] ≥ f(E[X]) for convex f, which in English means the expected payoff of an option is at least the payoff of the expected price. Randomness has strictly positive value to the holder.

That is the whole reason more volatility makes an option worth more, and notice what the argument did not use: any view on direction. Compare a share, whose payoff is the straight line S_T. Linear, so Jensen is an equality, so volatility adds nothing to its expected value. Curvature is the entire difference, and it is why the sanity check "vol up means price up, for calls and puts both" is a statement about the model being coherent rather than about the market.

Section 2

The stock: geometric Brownian motion

dS = μ·S·dt + σ·S·dW

Term by term. μ·S·dt is the drift: deterministic, proportional to today's price and to elapsed time. σ·S·dW is the diffusion: random, also proportional to today's price. dW is the Brownian increment, mean zero, variance dt, independent across non-overlapping intervals.

Every term carries S, which is what makes it geometric rather than arithmetic. Divide through and dS/S = μ·dt + σ·dW: the return is what has constant statistics, not the dollar move. A $500 stock and a $5 stock carry the same percentage risk.

Two consequences fall straight out. S can never go negative, and it can never reach zero from above in finite time, because the shock size shrinks with the price. That is a feature until it is a catastrophe: when WTI crude settled at minus $37.63 in April 2020, a lognormal model assigned probability zero to the state that had just occurred, so it could not price it at all. CME moved oil options settlement to Bachelier's arithmetic model, where negative prices are just the left tail, within days. Model choice is not aesthetics.

Section 3

Ito's lemma, and the one odd fact (dW)² = dt

This is not an algebraic identity and asserting it is how people lose the thread. It is a statement about quadratic variation, and it is provable in three lines.

Over a small interval ΔW ~ Normal(0, Δt), so E[(ΔW)²] = Δt exactly and Var[(ΔW)²] = 2(Δt)². Now sum the squared increments over n = T/Δt steps. The mean of the sum is T. The variance of the sum is 2·T·Δt, which goes to zero as Δt goes to zero.

So the sum of squared random increments converges to a non-random number. The randomness cancels in the square. By the same argument dt·dt = 0 and dt·dW = 0, since those vanish faster.

Here is why that matters: a second-order term normally dies in a Taylor expansion. This one survives at first order in dt. For a random process the second derivative sits in the drift. Curvature is not a correction term, it is a cash flow.

Taylor expand any smooth V(S, t) in two variables and substitute:

dV = V_t·dt + V_S·dS + ½·V_SS·(dS)² + ... (dS)² = μ²S²(dt)² + 2μσS²·dt·dW + σ²S²(dW)² = σ²S²·dt (first two terms die) dV = (V_t + μ·S·V_S + ½·σ²·S²·V_SS)·dt + σ·S·V_S·dW

Ordinary calculus would have given only the first two terms in that bracket. The extra ½σ²S²V_SS is the Ito correction, and it is pure gamma. That term is where an option owner's money comes from.

One payoff for having done it: apply the lemma to f = ln(S) and you get d(ln S) = (μ - σ²/2)dt + σdW, so S_T is lognormal. That minus σ²/2 is not a fudge, it is the Ito correction, and it is why the median path sits below the average path even with the drift unchanged.

Section 4

Hedging, and where mu goes

Build a portfolio: hold the option, short Δ shares against it.

Π = V - Δ·S, then choose Δ = ∂V/∂S

That choice is not arbitrary. V(S) is a curve, Δ is the slope of the tangent line at today's S, and shorting Δ shares subtracts that tangent line. What is left is pure curvature.

Now add the pieces and watch the random terms:

from Ito: dV = (V_t + μSV_S + ½σ²S²V_SS)dt + σSV_S·dW from the stock: -Δ·dS = -V_S·(μS·dt + σS·dW) +σSV_S·dW and -σSV_S·dW cancel exactly +μSV_S·dt and -μSV_S·dt cancel exactly dΠ = (V_t + ½σ²S²V_SS)dt <- no dW anywhere

This portfolio's value over the next instant is deterministic. Then one economic axiom, the only one used in the whole derivation: an instantaneously riskless portfolio must earn exactly the riskless rate, or you borrow at r, buy it, and print money. Set dΠ = rΠdt and rearrange:

V_t + r·S·V_S + ½·σ²·S²·V_SS - r·V = 0

μ is gone. The expected return of the stock, the single hardest quantity in finance to estimate and the thing every discretionary investor argues about, is not in the pricing equation. Mechanically it entered twice with opposite signs, once through the option's own drift and once through the shares you are short, and choosing Δ = V_S made the two coefficients identical.

Economically: two traders who disagree completely about direction must still agree on this price, or one of them can be arbitraged. The price is enforced by replication, not by opinion. And notice what replaced μ: the risk-free rate. That is risk-neutral valuation appearing for the first time. Nobody is claiming the stock drifts at r. The claim is that because the risk is perfectly hedgeable, the price is the same as it would be in a world where it did.

What survived is σ, through V_SS. Volatility and curvature are the only things you are actually paid for.

Worth flagging the assumption doing the heavy lifting: Δ is held fixed over the instant dt and then recomputed, which means continuous, costless rebalancing. That is the first thing to fail in reality, and it is what broke LTCM in 1998 even though the model was fine. See section 13.

Section 6

d₁ and d₂

d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r - q + σ²/2)·T] / (σ√T) d₂ = d₁ - σ√T

Read d₂ as the number of standard deviations by which the log-forward exceeds log(K), so Φ(d₂) is the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money. d₁ is the same quantity measured under the measure where the stock itself is the numeraire, which is why Φ(d₁) turns up as delta and is not a probability, despite living between 0 and 1.

Section 7

The price

call = S·e^(-qT)·Φ(d₁) - K·e^(-rT)·Φ(d₂) put = K·e^(-rT)·Φ(-d₂) - S·e^(-qT)·Φ(-d₁)

Both have the same shape: a discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure. The first term is the present value of receiving the stock in the states where the option is exercised, the second the present value of paying the strike in those same states.

The degenerate branch is worth a look rather than a skim. At T = 0, or with σ = 0, the formulas divide by zero, and both cases have exact answers that need no normal distribution at all. Returning those explicitly is the difference between a pricer that handles the edge of its own domain and one that returns NaN and lets it propagate silently into a greek.

The debugging check that matters: put-call parity. C - P = S·e^(-qT) - K·e^(-rT), a pure no-arbitrage identity with no model in it whatsoever, since a long call plus a short put is a synthetic long forward. It must hold to machine precision. If it does not, there is a sign error, and parity is what tells you so.

Section 8

The greeks

Nothing new is introduced here. The greeks are partial derivatives of the same surface V(S, t; σ, r), and the only thing being added is economic names. The calculus is the multivariable chain rule and a second-order Taylor expansion.

One structural fact worth carrying: gamma, vega, vanna and volga are identical for a call and a put. That is not a coincidence to memorise, it follows from parity. The difference C - P is linear in S and has no σ in it at all, so any derivative of that difference which is either second order or higher in S, or touches σ even once, is zero. Gamma is the first kind, vega and volga the second, vanna both. The suite asserts it.

Note the wording: "the second derivatives vanish" is the tidier sentence and it does not cover vega, which is only a first derivative. It is still identical across calls and puts, and the reason is the missing σ rather than the linearity in S.

Section 9

How I know the greeks are right

This is the part I would want asked. A greek formula typed from memory with one wrong sign still returns numbers that look completely plausible, and no amount of re-reading it reliably catches that. Staring at the formula is not a check.

So every analytic greek is asserted equal to a central finite difference of the price function, across eight parameter sets and both option kinds. The chain of trust runs: published benchmark verifies price(), price() differentiated numerically verifies greeks().

And then the tests themselves were mutation-tested. A passing test proves nothing until you have watched it fail, so each formula was deliberately broken one at a time to confirm that a specific test goes red: theta's sign flipped, charm's sign flipped, vanna's sign flipped, volga's indices swapped, gamma's dividend discount dropped, vega's √T removed, d₂ built with a plus, the arbitrage assertion deleted, the risk-neutral probability inverted, d set to something other than 1/u, the per-step discount dropped, the terminal exponent shifted, and the up and down children swapped.

All were caught except one, and that one was a real finding rather than a formality: the exercise-boundary test counted flags across every slice including the terminal slice, whose flags are set by a different expression. Killing every interior flag still left the count positive, so the test was green for a reason that had nothing to do with what it claimed to check. It has been narrowed to interior nodes, and the narrowed version kills the mutation.

Section 10

The lattice, and where q comes from

Same economics as the PDE, in finite steps you can actually look at. Over one period the stock does exactly one of two things. Build a portfolio of Δ shares plus B in bonds and demand it match the option in both states:

Δ·u·S + B·e^(r·dt) = V_u Δ·d·S + B·e^(r·dt) = V_d subtract: Δ = (V_u - V_d) / (S·(u - d)) <- this is delta, discretely solve, substitute, and it collapses to: V = e^(-r·dt)·[ q·V_u + (1 - q)·V_d ], q = (e^((r-q_div)·dt) - d) / (u - d)

q was never assumed. Nobody estimated a probability and nobody expressed a view. It fell out of solving two linear equations in two unknowns. It happens to behave like a probability, and it is the risk-neutral measure in its simplest possible form: check that q·u + (1-q)·d = e^((r-q_div)·dt), so under q the stock grows at exactly the risk-free rate net of the dividend yield. Not because it does, but because that is the measure under which hedged prices are consistent. With no dividend that is just the risk-free rate, which is the case worth carrying in your head, but the dividend term is in the code and it belongs here too.

The real-world probability of an up move appears nowhere, which is the same phenomenon as μ vanishing from the PDE, seen a second time in a much simpler setting.

q lies strictly in (0,1) if and only if d < e^(r·dt) < u. That is not a technical footnote, it is no-arbitrage written in three symbols: outside it the stock either dominates the bond in both states or is dominated in both, and a static arbitrage exists. So it is asserted in code before any pricing happens, and the error message says what to change.

On u and d: CRR uses u = e^(σ√dt) and d = 1/u. The √dt is the same square-root-of-time scaling as dW and it makes the tree's variance per step match σ²dt to leading order. The d = 1/u choice makes the tree recombine, so n steps give n+1 terminal nodes instead of 2ⁿ. That single choice is the difference between a program that runs and one that does not.

Section 11

Backward induction

Start at expiry, where the value is known exactly because it is just the payoff. Step backwards. At each node the continuation value is the discounted risk-neutral expectation of the two children.

The delta can also be read straight off the first branching, the replication way rather than by bumping the input. This is not a finite difference bolted on afterwards, it is literally the share count the replicating portfolio holds, which is where the continuous-time delta came from in the first place. That it lands on Φ(d₁) is the discrete and continuous arguments agreeing.

Section 12

Early exercise

Exactly one line changes: at each node, take max(intrinsic, continuation). That single comparison converts the whole thing from European to American, and it is the optimal stopping problem solved by dynamic programming. The exercise boundary is never specified anywhere. It emerges from a few thousand comparisons.

Merton's theorem is the unit test. An American call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised early, and the proof needs no model at all. Exercising gives exactly S - K. Selling gives at least S - K·e^(-rT). Since r > 0 and T > 0, K·e^(-rT) < K, so selling strictly beats exercising, always. Two separate reasons underneath: exercising hands over the strike sooner than necessary and forfeits the interest on it, and it throws away the protection if the stock later falls back below K.

Where it does bite is puts, because exercising hands you K in cash now and that cash earns r. Deep in the money the remaining optionality is nearly worthless while the interest is real. That is also why the sanity check works: as r goes to zero the entire benefit disappears and the American put collapses onto the European one.

Note the choice to difference on a single lattice rather than against the closed form. The discretisation error is common to both legs and cancels, so what is left is the early-exercise value rather than that value plus a few basis points of lattice noise.

Section 13

Where this model breaks

Worth being straight about, because the interesting question is never whether the model is wrong. It is.

1987. The S&P fell about 20.5% in a session, a log return of about -0.229. At 1.26% daily volatility, which is 20% annualised, that is roughly an 18 sigma move. Under a normal distribution P(Z < -18) is small enough that you would expect to wait vastly longer than the age of the universe. The model does not merely underestimate that event, it assigns it probability zero for practical purposes. The failure is in the tails: lognormality is a claim about tail thinness, and real returns have fat tails, jumps, and volatility that clusters. The centre of the distribution was fine, and the centre is not what kills you.

The market's response never went away. Before 1987 implied volatilities across strikes were roughly flat, which is what you would expect if everyone believed the model. Since then out-of-the-money puts trade at permanently higher implied vols. Read that carefully: it does not mean traders think vol is genuinely higher at low strikes. It means the model is wrong and they are using its single free parameter to force the formula to output the price they actually want to charge. Every strike carries its own σ.

1998. LTCM, with Merton and Scholes on the board, is a different failure and the distinction matters. 1987 was the model's distribution being wrong. 1998 was the model being fine while the assumptions around it, continuous trading, infinite liquidity, no financing constraints, were wrong. Both are the same line from section 4 failing: you cannot always rebalance the hedge, and you least can exactly when you most need to.

What this means for the pricer. It is correct inside its assumptions and the assumptions are false. That is not a reason to distrust the output, it is a reason to know which number is doing what. On a desk today nobody feeds σ in to get a price. They observe the price and solve for the σ that reproduces it, and that number, the implied volatility, is the real object. BSM survives as an interface, a shared convention for quoting in vol and reporting risk, rather than as a belief about how stocks move. A wrong model everyone agrees on beats a right model nobody shares.
